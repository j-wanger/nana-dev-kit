# SEASONAL KITCHEN PRINCIPLES — Apply these to every cooking decision

## PRINCIPLE-001: Taste Before Seasoning
**Always:** Establish a flavor baseline before adding anything. Taste the raw ingredients first. Understand what the dish already brings before reaching for salt, acid, or heat.
**Never:** Season based on habit alone. Skip tasting between additions. Declare a dish finished without a final taste check.
**Common mistakes to avoid:**
- Salting without tasting first (oversalting is irreversible — you cannot extract dissolved sodium from a braise)
- Following recipe measurements exactly without adjusting for ingredient variation (tomato acidity varies by season)
- Adding acid and salt simultaneously (each masks the other — season one dimension at a time)

## PRINCIPLE-002: Check the Pantry Before Shopping
**Always:** Inventory what you already have. Check refrigerator drawers, freezer stock, and dry storage first. Ask "can I make this with what exists?"
**Never:** Buy new ingredients without checking existing stock. Assume you are out of something without looking. Justify a store trip with "I probably need more" without verifying actual quantities.
**Common mistakes to avoid:**
- Purchasing duplicate staples that expire before use (average household wastes significant purchased food by weight)
- Buying specialty items for a single recipe when a pantry substitute exists (dried herbs replace fresh in most cooked applications)
- Treating "not the exact brand" as insufficient when the functional ingredient is identical

## PRINCIPLE-003: Prep at the Boundary, Trust the Process
**Always:** Complete all cutting, measuring, and mise en place before heat is applied. Organize ingredients by addition order. Once cooking begins, trust the sequence.
**Never:** Chop while sautéing. Measure flour while the roux is browning. Interrupt a rising dough to add forgotten ingredients.
**Common mistakes to avoid:**
- Starting to cook before all vegetables are cut (leads to uneven sizes from rushed knife work)
- Checking oven temperature repeatedly (opening the door drops temperature and disrupts convection)
- Adding cold ingredients to hot oil because prep was not completed (temperature shock causes splatter)

## PRINCIPLE-004: The Simpler Dish Usually Tastes Better
**Always:** Default to fewer ingredients with higher quality. Apply the removal test — would this dish suffer if you removed one component? Prefer three excellent ingredients over seven mediocre ones.
**Never:** Complicate a dish for presentation alone. Add garnishes without tasting them in combination. Justify elaborate technique with "it looks more professional." Confuse "less effort" with "simpler flavor" — reducing a stock for hours concentrates flavor even though it requires more time.
**Common mistakes to avoid:**
- Adding truffle oil to compensate for bland base ingredients (aromatic additions cannot fix structural flavor deficits)
- Following complex multi-component plating when family-style service would showcase the food better
- Over-seasoning to mask stale spices rather than replacing the spice drawer (ground spices lose potency within months of opening)

## PRINCIPLE-005: Make Spoilage Visible, Not Hidden
**Always:** Store ingredients with dates facing forward. Label every container with contents and date. Distinguish intentional fermentation from accidental spoilage.
**Never:** Push older items behind newer ones. Ignore off-odors because the use-by date has not passed. Store leftovers in opaque containers without labels. Treat all expiration dates identically.
**Common mistakes to avoid:**
- Stacking new purchases on top of older inventory (first-in-first-out prevents waste but requires shelf discipline)
- Serving dairy that smells acceptable but has been open past the printed date without a taste check (odor is a lagging indicator)
- Freezing items without noting the date or contents (unlabeled frozen items become unusable after three months)
